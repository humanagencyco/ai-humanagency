Evals · Explained

What is an eval?

An eval is a test you run on an AI system to check whether its answers are good enough. It is the AI version of a school exam: the same questions, marked the same way, so you can see whether things are getting better or worse.

In one line: an eval = a set of realistic questions + a way to mark each answer as pass or fail + a score you track over time.

Why AI needs evals

A normal calculator gives the same answer to 2 + 2 every time, so you can check it once and trust it. An AI can answer the same question in different words each time, and some of those answers are wrong in subtle ways. You cannot check it once and forget it.

Evals are how you keep checking, in a consistent way, as you change the prompt, the model, or the product. Quality becomes a score you track, not a green tick you get once.

An everyday example

Suppose you run a small online shop and use AI to answer customer emails about delivery.

  • The questions: 30 real emails from the past month, such as "Where is my parcel?" and "Can I change my address after ordering?"
  • The marking rule: each reply passes only if it gives the correct delivery time from the policy, and does not promise a refund that the policy does not allow.
  • The score: 24 of 30 pass, so 80%.

Next month you change the prompt. You run the same 30 emails again. If the score goes to 90%, the change helped. If it drops to 60%, you know to undo it, and you know why.

The three parts of every eval

  1. 1QuestionsRealistic inputs. Start with 20 to 50, taken from real use where you can.
  2. 2MarkingA rule that says pass or fail for each answer.
  3. 3ScoreThe percentage that passed. Track it over time.

Two kinds of eval

Model evals

Public tests and leaderboards, such as MMLU or SWE-bench. They help you shortlist 2 or 3 models. They say little about your own use.

Product evals

Your own tests, built from your own requests and failures. They tell you whether your product actually works for your users. This is where the real work is.

Three ways to mark an answer

  1. 1Code checksFor hard rules: valid format, required fields, length, banned words. Fast, cheap and always consistent. Code can't judge meaning, though.
  2. 2An AI judgeA second model marks each answer against a written rule, for things like tone or sticking to the facts. Fast at scale, but it can be wrong too, so check it against people.
  3. 3PeopleSomeone who knows the subject marks a sample by hand. Slow, but it's how you decide what "good" means in the first place.

Most teams use all three: code for the rules, a judge for quality, people to set and recheck the standard.

Start by reading real answers

The most common mistake is to pick a score, such as "helpfulness out of 5", before you know what goes wrong. The method that works, as taught by Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar:

  1. 1Collect real examples.Record what the system did, not only the final answer.
  2. 2Note the first thing that went wrong in each.One short note each, such as "invented a refund policy" or "ignored the postcode".
  3. 3Group the notes and count them.Each group is a failure mode. The biggest one is where to start.
  4. 4Write one eval per failure mode you care about.Step 3 shows how to write the marking rule well.

Reading 30 real outputs by hand usually teaches you more than a week of picking metrics.

The eval loop

Read real answers → name the failures → write an eval for each
     ↑                                             ↓
Change the prompt, tools or model  ←  Run the evals, see what breaks

Every new failure you find in real use becomes a permanent test. That's how yesterday's problems stop coming back.

What an eval is not

  • Not a one-off check. You run it again after every change.
  • Not a vibe. "It seems better" is not an eval. A written pass/fail rule is.
  • Not a public leaderboard. Those help choose a model. Your own evals tell you whether your product works.
